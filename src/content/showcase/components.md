A single part is only useful if it works with the parts around it. This group comes from two products: a **C-clamp** (frame, screw, anvil, and pin) and a **machinist's vise** (jaw plate, C clip, pins, drilled ball, and a flat-point set screw). Each component is modeled to its real size, so that a pin fits its hole, a screw passes through its frame, and the parts can be mated into an assembly that actually moves. The C-clamp parts come together in the [C1000 assembly drawing](/cad111/drawings/c1000-drawing).

Getting there meant researching each piece of hardware before modeling it: what a flat-point set screw is for, why a C clip needs an open slot, and where each pin and cross hole lines up in the finished product. Each part also gets a **part number and a spec table**, recording its overall size in millimeters and inches so the numbers can be checked against the drawing and against the parts it mates with.

**Why it matters on the job:** when a machine is assembled or repaired, mismatched sizes and fits are a common source of failures. Modeling components to verified dimensions, and keeping that information organized by part number, is how engineers catch interference and clearance problems on screen before any metal is cut. That is troubleshooting done early, when fixing it is cheapest, and it is the kind of data management that keeps a product's parts interchangeable.
